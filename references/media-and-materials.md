# Teaching Media and Materials: Choosing, Designing, Using

Sources: *Teaching Media* lecture notes (Nguyen Minh Trung, Vinh Long University of Technology Education); part 2 of the *Teaching Methodology* textbook (HCMC University of Technology and Education, 2007); Module 03 - Preparing to Teach.

## 1. Selection principle

The decision chain is: **purpose -> content -> method -> media**. The method guides the choice of media. Media are never decoration.

The objective decides the type of media. The textbook's original example: the objective "install a pump" needs a real pump; "explain how the pump works" needs a cutaway model; "explain how pressure changes" is served by one drawing. Applied to other fields: the objective "present to a live audience" needs a real room or group and a camera to record it; "build a slide deck" needs a computer and a slide template; "tell three types of opening apart" needs only 3 short clips or 3 script excerpts.

Six factors to weigh: the objective; the content and method; the learners' characteristics; the facilities; the instructor's skills; the room space and lighting (or the online platform).

The effectiveness pyramid (weakest to strongest): plain talk -> board, pictures, models -> film, slides, video -> **learners practicing, working individually, doing projects, going on field visits**. Retention rates: hearing 20%, seeing 30%, hearing + seeing 50%, hearing + seeing + saying it back 70%, **adding doing: 90%**. Conclusion for lesson plans: every piece of media you bring out must come with **something the learners do with it**.

## 2. Principles of use: the 3 "rights", plus safety and fit

- **Right time**: bring it out when learners are expecting it (after the lead-in and the problem are set), and put it away when done. Do not lay out a pile of media at the start of the session. Show a film at the end of the session or exactly where it is needed, and do not screen several pieces in a row.
- **Right place**: the back row and the two rows next to the walls must see clearly. Everything should be readable from 8 m. Have a place to store items in the room.
- **Right intensity**: effectiveness drops when one type of activity runs past 15 minutes. Keep audiovisual use to 3-4 times a week at most, 20-25 minutes per session at most, and break video about every 10 minutes to ask a question or consolidate.
- **Before use there must be a lead-in; after use there must be an activity** (a question, an exercise, practice). Media never stand alone.

## 3. Slides (PowerPoint, Canva, Google Slides)

Design rules:
- **One main idea per slide**; more ideas means more slides.
- **Maximum 6 bullet lines**, following the 6x6 rule or the 8x8 rule (lines x words per line). Text is headings and outline, never a transcript of the lecture.
- **Maximum 2 fonts and 3 text colors**. Assign colors by function and keep them consistent (question, definition, remember, answer). Keep the background uniform, with no color change from slide to slide and no glaring colors.
- Turn text into a **block diagram** instead of a list of lines. Graphics must serve the main idea.
- Do not overuse effects. Use "dim what has been said" or a gradual reveal to focus attention.
- Slides **support instructor-learner and learner-learner interaction**; they are not there so the instructor can talk to the machine.
- Include question or pause slides exactly where the lesson plan calls for them, and a summary slide for each part and at the end.

A 6-step process for building a digital lesson: objective -> pick the core knowledge -> make it multimedia (images, diagrams, clips, audio) -> build a structured resource bank -> assemble slides following the *cognitive activities* (not the table of contents of the book) -> run it through and check the links.

In the lesson content file, write a **slide outline** as a table: slide number | title | main content (max 6 lines) | image or diagram | presenting notes (pause, ask, reveal gradually).

## 4. The board (chalk, whiteboard, online whiteboard)

Divide it into 3 zones: **left** holds the outline and stays fixed all session; **middle** is for explaining and drawing, erased often; **right** holds keywords, formulas and the space where learners work. Use big letters and bold strokes, with the lesson title largest. Sketch complex figures roughly or print them in advance. Keep your talk in step with what appears on the board. At the end of the session the main zone should still be intact for the summary. Reveal parts in sequence, and invite learners up to the board.

The lesson plan carries a "board plan": what goes in the left zone, what is drawn in the middle zone during which activity, and what sits in the right zone.

## 5. Handouts and printed materials

Four types: *information sheets* (facts, hard-to-find concepts, updates); *task sheets* (the problem, questions, what to do, reference sources); *job sheets* (a complete procedure, tools, safety, diagram); *practice guides* (step by step, how to use the tools).

When you need one: new information not in the textbook; complex content; a summary by topic; learners struggle when carrying out a skill; you want to cut down on note-taking.

Requirements: a clear purpose; a title; definitions of new terms; images or diagrams to complement the words; not text-heavy, with wide margins; emphasis through bold, numbers or bullets; consistent terminology; suggestions for further reading. A task sheet must state the **deadline and the marking criteria**. A job sheet must contain a **tool list, step-by-step instructions and safety notes**.

Template for an individual task sheet: Topic / Performance objective / Requirements / Performance criteria / Deadline. Template for a job sheet: Topic / Objective / Job / Procedure / Time / Materials / Result / Standard.

## 6. Posters, cards and flipcharts (live sessions, workshops)

- Posters and wall charts: one idea per chart; letters at least 2 cm tall; at most 2 fonts; at most 3 colors (black, blue and red are the easiest to read); 6 words x 6 lines; size A2 or larger. Use gradual reveal, gradual posting, and covered captions for checking.
- Colored cards (pinboard, groups of 15-25): **one idea per card, at most 3 lines, not all capitals, same type means same color**. Let the cards appear one by one. Compare two groups of cards to choose the best ideas.
- Flipchart: at most 7 lines; not all capitals; after writing, turn to face the audience; do not read from the paper, use it as support.

## 7. Real objects, models, video, field visits

- Real objects: use them when no other medium conveys the point better, and always attach observation instructions: state the purpose -> general features -> how the details work -> stress the key points -> conclude. For instructors of instructors, "real objects" are usually a real lesson plan, a real slide deck, a recording of a real class or a real assessment form.
- Models: a substitute when the object is too large, too small or too abstract. Three steps: place it where everyone can see -> state the purpose and how to observe -> observe, analyze the parts, conclude.
- Video: 5-20 minutes; preview it yourself; **pose the questions you want the film to answer before you play it**; pause at the key points; hold a discussion or exercise straight after viewing.
- Field visits and class observation: prepare the purpose, the points to watch and the questions; run the visit with discipline; wrap up with a discussion or report.

## 8. Online live classes and self-paced video courses

The principles stay the same; the media change names: board -> screen share or digital whiteboard; handout -> downloadable file or form; group -> breakout room or submitted assignment; demonstration -> screen recording with voiceover; real object -> showing the instructor's actual working documents, on any platform (a live video tool, a learning management system, or a course marketplace such as Udemy).

For a self-paced video course: each video runs at most 10-12 minutes and covers one unit of knowledge, with its own opening, body and close. After every 1-2 videos, give learners something to do (a quiz, a submitted assignment, a checklist). Downloadable resources replace the paper handout. Learners have no instructor beside them, so **the script must carry its own questions and pauses** ("Pause the video and write down...").

## 9. Writing it into the lesson plan

The "Preparation / Conditions" section lists media in groups: equipment (projector, board, camera, online platform); instructor materials (a slide deck of n slides, clips, real objects, models); learner materials (task sheets, handouts, templates). Each activity in the lesson-flow table states which medium is used, when, and what the learners do with it.
