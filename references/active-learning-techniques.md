# Active Learning Techniques: Quick Reference for Building Activities

Source: the *Active Teaching Techniques* compilation (based on Nguyen Van Cuong, Vietnam Upper Secondary Education Development Project, 2006), 32 techniques. A technique is **the smallest unit of a teaching method**: one activity in a lesson plan is usually one lead method plus 1-2 techniques. Choose techniques by position in the lesson and by group size, then write all 7 elements of a task brief (section 4).

## 1. Choose by position in the lesson

| Position | Suitable techniques |
|---|---|
| Warm-up, activating prior knowledge, building motivation | Brainstorming; Lightning round; KWL (columns K, W); We know 3; Active writing / quick write; Creative grouping; Kipling 5W1H |
| Building new knowledge | Jigsaw; Placemat; Carousel; Active reading; Video analysis; Mind map; Ask the expert; Problem solving; Questioning; Think-Pair-Share |
| Practice and application | Role play; Games; Pro-con debate; Gallery walk; Inside-outside circle; Say it another way; Group summary; Question chain |
| Consolidation, end-of-lesson feedback | One-minute paper; 3-3-3 feedback; Active writing / quick write; KWL (columns L, H); Feedback |

## 2. Choose by group size

- **Individual**: Active writing, Active reading, Kipling 5W1H, One-minute paper, KWL.
- **Pairs**: Think-Pair-Share, Inside-outside circle.
- **Small groups of 3-6**: Placemat (4-6), 6-3-5 brainwriting (6 people x 3 ideas x 5 minutes), We know 3 (3 people, 10 minutes), Jigsaw, Carousel, Mind map, Brainstorming.
- **Whole class**: Lightning round, Fishbowl, Pro-con debate, Gallery walk, Ask the expert, Question chain.

## 3. Short description of each technique (enough to write into a lesson plan)

**Brainstorming** - Split into groups and state a clear problem. Every idea is recorded and *criticism is banned* while collecting. Afterwards merge duplicates, drop off-topic ideas and pick the best. Fast and easy; risk of drifting off topic if the prompt is vague.

**Lightning round** - Each person says 1-2 sentences, very fast, on one question. Discuss only after everyone has spoken. Use it to take the pulse of the room or change the atmosphere.

**6-3-5 brainwriting (XYZ)** - 6 people each write 3 ideas in 5 minutes, then pass the sheet to the neighbor. It forces everyone to contribute; synthesizing takes time.

**Placemat** - A large sheet (A0 or flipchart) is divided into sections. Each person writes ideas in their own corner, and the group agrees on shared ideas in the center. Builds individual accountability.

**Jigsaw** - Round 1, "expert groups": each group takes one topic and *every member must be able to present it*. Round 2, regroup so each new group holds one expert per topic, and each person teaches their part. Topics must be independent of each other; do not use for content linked by cause and effect.

**Carousel** - Each group gets one question and writes on a large sheet. Sheets rotate around the room so other groups can add to them until each returns to its original group, which completes the sheet and posts it on the wall.

**Gallery walk** - Individuals or groups put ideas on card or paper and stick them on the wall. The whole class walks around, views and comments. Collect the best options.

**Fishbowl** - One group sits in the middle and discusses, while the others sit in an outer ring, observe and give comments afterwards. An empty chair can let an outsider step in with a contribution. Needs space and decent acoustics.

**Inside-outside circle** - Two concentric circles face each other. Pairs talk for a few minutes, then the inner circle rotates to create new pairs.

**Think-Pair-Share** - Think alone, talk in pairs, then share with another pair or the class. Model it so learners share *their partner's idea*, not only their own.

**Kipling 5W1H** - Who, What, Where, When, How, Why. Keep the questions short and on topic. Works for individuals too.

**Mind map** - Branch out from a central idea using images, symbols and keywords. Let learners draw their own (a map the instructor draws in advance is remembered less well). Good for review and for linking theory to practice.

**KWL/KWLH** - A table with 3-4 columns: already know, want to know, learned, how to learn more. Fill in K and W before the lesson and L after it. Supports self-directed learning.

**Active reading** - Give guiding questions first. Each person skims, predicts, finds the main ideas and summarizes. Share in groups of 2-4, then ask the instructor about whatever is still unclear. Saves time on content that is plentiful but not difficult.

**Video analysis** - A clip of 5-20 minutes, previewed by the instructor. Pose the questions *before* playing. Watch, then learners work alone or in pairs to answer or summarize.

**Questioning** - Tied to the objective. Short and clear, well timed, pitched at the right level, easy to hard. *No chained questions and no several-issues-at-once questions.*

**Problem solving** - 8 steps: identify the problem, gather information, list possible solutions, analyze each, compare, choose, implement, reflect. The situation should be familiar, of moderate length, contain a conflict and allow several solutions. It can be delivered as text, an image or a short skit. Use brainstorming at the "list solutions" step.

**Role play** - State the topic, hand out the situation, set time limits for preparing and performing. Groups prepare, then perform. **The class then discusses the behavior and feelings shown (the main part)**, and the instructor concludes. Do not give a ready-made script or lines. The situation must allow several solutions. Encourage shy learners. Keep props simple.

**Games** - Announce the name and rules, do a trial run, play, evaluate, then *discuss the learning point*. Easy to run, safe, within the time, and learners help run it.

**Project (mini)** - Plan (choose the topic, subtopics, tasks), carry out (collect, survey, consult), then synthesize (product, presentation, reflection). The product must be presentable.

**Task assignment** - See section 4.

**Ask the expert** - A group of "experts" researches in advance and sits at the front. The class asks, the experts answer, and a moderator runs it.

**Pro-con debate** - Choose a topic with real conflict. Two groups gather arguments, then take turns: one argument, one rebuttal. With fewer than 6 per group everyone speaks. The aim is to see many sides, not to "win".

**Question chain** - The person who just answered asks the next question to someone else. It stops when the instructor decides. Used for consolidation.

**Say it another way** - Groups list 10 unhelpful things people say about a situation, then find 10 more positive ways to say them. Discuss the meaning. Suits lessons on communication, feedback and attitude.

**Group summary** - The group reads a text aloud, discusses it and presents the main points. Members answer the class's questions.

**We know 3** - Groups of 3, 10 minutes. Choose the 3 most important things already known about the topic and send a representative to present.

**Active writing / quick write** - In the middle of a talk, the instructor asks a question and gives a few minutes of free writing, then invites a few people to read aloud. Also used at the end of a lesson to summarize and collect feedback.

**One-minute paper** - At the end (or middle) of a lesson: "What is the most important thing you learned today? What is the most important question still unanswered?" Write it down, then each person speaks for 1 minute.

**3-3-3 feedback** - Each person writes 3 good things, 3 things that were not good and 3 suggestions about content or method. Collect, process and discuss.

**Feedback** - Positive feedback is empathetic, controlled, specific, timely, free of value judgments and convertible into action. Rules: speak briefly and in sequence; understand before commenting; look for causes; offer options.

**Grouping methods** - Count off by number, color, flower or season; match puzzle pieces; group by interest (a Painters group, a Poets group, a Debaters group); by birth month; by same level or mixed level. Vary the method to keep interest up and encourage mixing.

## 4. The 7 elements every task brief must cover

Every group or individual activity in a lesson plan must answer all of these. If one is missing, the activity gets muddled in the room:

1. **Who**: individual, pair, or group of how many, and how are groups formed?
2. **What**: what is the task (one clear instruction with a verb)?
3. **Where**: in place, around the room, or in an online breakout?
4. **How long**: how many minutes, and when will you call out the time remaining?
5. **With what**: handout, A0 sheet, colored markers, link, which pages of which document?
6. **Product**: what must exist at the end (a diagram, 3 ideas on cards, a 100-word paragraph, a 3-minute role play)?
7. **How presented or assessed**: who presents, for how many minutes, by which criteria, and who judges?

The task must fit the activity objective, the learners' level, the time, the space and the equipment available.

## 5. Rules for writing scenarios for role play and problem solving

- Keep them close to the learners' real work: classrooms and parents for teachers, wards and handovers for nurses, tickets and angry customers for support agents, sprint reviews for developers.
- Include a conflict or a decision to make. Allow **several solutions**; no single obvious answer.
- Moderate length (80-200 words), with enough facts to act on. A picture or skit can accompany it.
- Provide no script or lines; learners build them.
- The discussion after the performance matters more than the performance: prepare 3-4 guiding questions and the instructor's steering conclusion in advance.
