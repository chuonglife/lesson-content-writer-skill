# Natural Writing Style: Removing the AI Smell

A lesson has to read as if a real instructor, flaws and habits included, wrote it for their own class. Readers spot the "AI smell" within a few lines and stop trusting the text. Use this file in the final review pass: reread all of `02_Lesson_Content.md` (and the prose parts of `01_Learning_Objectives.md`), fix every violation, then read one random paragraph aloud.

The six rule groups below were originally written by course creator Chuong for his own course production (2026). The notes show how to apply them to lesson content.

## 1. Symbols and structures to remove

- **Em dash and en dash**: banned entirely, as punctuation. Replace with a hyphen "-", a comma, a period, a colon or a line break. In tables use " | " or split the sentence. Ranges take a plain hyphen: "5-10 minutes". In a "Question ... Answer" pair, write two sentences or two lines.
- **Stock phrases and AI vocabulary**: delve, tapestry, testament, "in today's fast-paced world", "in an ever-changing landscape", "it's worth noting", "not only... but also", "plays a crucial role", "plays a vital role", "let's dive in", "let's explore", "unlock", "elevate", "embark", "journey", "seamless", "robust", "leverage", "foster", "empower", "navigate the complexities", "game-changer", "in conclusion", "that's why", "Great question!", "it is important to remember", "I hope this lesson helps".
- **Rule-of-three adjectives** ("fast, simple, effective"): AI loves triplets, people rarely talk that way. No more than one triplet per page. Break it into 2, into 4, or turn it into a sentence.
- **A repeated opening mold** (rhetorical question, then framing, then content): a lesson must not open like the previous one in the same course. Read the lead-ins of the lessons already written before writing a new one, and vary the approach (see the lead-in options in `lesson-structure.md`).
- **Empty transitions** ("Now let's move on to..."), **redundant summary sentences** closing every section, and **a rhetorical question followed by its own answer**.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

- AI writes evenly long sentences with pauses that are too well placed. People write long and short in a jumble, with a stubby 3-4 word sentence between two long ones. Every paragraph of spoken script needs at least one sentence under 6 words.
- Read it aloud. If it sounds like a presentation or a report, rewrite it as if you were telling a colleague over coffee.
- Uneven paragraphs: some of one sentence, some of six.

## 3. Concrete detail instead of generalities

- Not "a client", "many years ago", "a teacher". Say "Maria, who teaches Year 9 science in Leeds", "back in March 2022", "a cohort of 41 trainees".
- Odd numbers, not round ones: "47% margin" is more believable than "50%"; "a 12-page lesson plan" beats "a very long lesson plan". Use odd numbers only in examples, stories and personal experience. Sourced pedagogical figures (the attention curve, speech rates and so on) stay exactly as the source gives them.
- Every concept gets one example with a name and a number. No real example yet, no moving on to the next section.
- Banned placeholders: "John Doe", "Company XYZ", "Subject X".

## 4. Personal and regional wording

- Drop in spoken phrasing, regional expressions and the writer's own way of saying things: "I usually call this...", "to be blunt", "the annoying part is", "where it falls apart is", "a bit of a mess", "dead simple". A few spots per lesson is enough, scattered unevenly.
- In the spoken script, slight departures from textbook grammar are allowed: starting with "And", "So", "But"; fragments with no subject; repeating a word for emphasis. Real people do not write perfectly.
- Administrative register ("implement", "conduct a review", "ensure", "utilize") is reserved for plans, sheets and criteria. In the spoken lecture use "do", "check again", "make sure".

## 5. Opinions and bias, not neutrality

- AI stays neutral and weighs both sides. A real instructor has biases, pet hates, and sometimes overstates out of irritation. Each lesson needs at least: one spot where "I hate / I can't stand...", one where "I got this wrong myself", and one where "the book says X, I do it differently".
- No unearned praise ("Awesome!", "Excellent!"), no motivational-speaker tone, no hedging ("may", "often", "generally") where a firm statement is needed. If you are not sure, say "I haven't verified this".
- Use "we" only when both of you really do the thing together. Otherwise it is "I" and "you".

## 6. Formatting

- No emoji, no icons.
- Little bold: in the lecture text of one section, at most 3 bold spots (a concept's first mention, an instruction to learners). Plans, tables and sheets may bold their labels.
- Real talk is not chopped into slide-like sections. The spoken script of each small section is written as running paragraphs. The labels Introduce / Present / Apply / Check / Lock-in are markers for the planner, kept to one word, never turned into big headings. No bullet points for something that is naturally one sentence.

## 7. Two voices in one file, never mixed

| Part | Voice | Sign it is right |
|---|---|---|
| Plan, timetable, sheets, criteria, quiz | Plain formal register, short sentences, verb first | No "I", no exclamations, no storytelling |
| Lecture script, lead-in, demonstration, consolidation | The spoken voice of one specific person | Has "I", spoken phrasing, a personal opinion, names and numbers |

A lecture goes wrong in voice when the "key teaching points" on the slide are copied into sentences. The slide says "6 levels, 2001, verbs"; the script has to say "In 2001 they revised this scale. Swapped every noun for a verb. Why? Because..."

## 8. Seven final tests (do all of them; note any deliberate exceptions under "Assumptions")

1. Search the file for the em dash and the en dash: the count must be 0.
2. Search for each phrase in group 1: every hit gets rewritten.
3. Read two random paragraphs aloud; where you run out of breath or sound like a report, cut the sentence and add a short one.
4. Count parallel triplets on one page; more than one, break them.
5. Count bold spots in the lecture text of one section; more than 3, remove some.
6. In each lecture section, look for a named person plus an odd number, and for one personal opinion (hate, mistake, doing it differently). Add what is missing.
7. Ask: could anyone have written this paragraph about any topic? If yes, it is not yet the lecture for this lesson.

## Writing in other languages

The same six groups apply in every language. Only the list of clichés changes, because each language has its own AI tells. Before writing in any language other than English, build the equivalent list for that language (the stock openers, the filler praise, the abstract nouns the model reaches for, the triplets, the dash habit) and run the final tests against it.

Example for Vietnamese output, taken from the original rule set: "không chỉ... mà còn", "trong thế giới ngày nay", "trong bối cảnh hiện nay", "không thể phủ nhận rằng", "đóng vai trò quan trọng", "hãy cùng khám phá", "hãy cùng tìm hiểu", "đó là lý do tại sao", "điều quan trọng là", "chìa khóa", "hành trình", "nâng tầm", "tối ưu hóa", "đột phá", "toàn diện", "góp phần", "nói tóm lại", "hy vọng bài học này", "chúc anh chị thành công". In Vietnamese the long dash is banned too, and Sino-Vietnamese administrative words ("thực hiện", "triển khai", "rà soát", "đảm bảo") belong in plans and sheets, not in the spoken lecture.
