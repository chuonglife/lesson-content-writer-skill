## Learning objectives

**By the end of this lesson, learners will be able to:**

### Knowledge
- Describe the 6 cognitive levels of the 2001 version of Bloom's taxonomy (Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create) in the correct order, with at least 3 action verbs for each level, without opening any material during the quiz, scoring at least 8/10. [K1]
- Tell correctly written objectives from badly written ones in a given list of 10 objectives (faults: unmeasurable verb, missing object, missing standard), naming the fault in each faulty objective correctly, at least 8/10. [K2]

### Skills
- Write at least 3 course-level objectives and 1 objective for each lesson in the structure map of their own course (made in M01), on the objective table provided, each objective with all 4 components (measurable action verb + object + condition + standard) and at the Bloom level planned for that lesson. [S1]
- Score their own objective table with the 6-criterion checklist provided, record the faults, and rewrite every objective that fails before submitting. [S2]

### Autonomy and responsibility
- Carefully remove every unmeasurable verb (understand, know, grasp, be aware of, become familiar with) from the submitted table, even when that takes several rewrites, and revise on the instructor's feedback within 1 week. [A1]

This lesson builds the component competency **"Write measurable objectives for a course and for each lesson"**, the first half of the module competency of M02 (write teaching objectives for a course and for each lesson using the correct action verbs of Bloom's taxonomy). ## 2. Topic introduction and just-enough theory

### 2.1 Video 2 - Bloom's taxonomy 2001: six levels of thinking (11 minutes)

**Key teaching points** (slides 6-9):
- Bloom's taxonomy 1956 → revision by Anderson and Krathwohl, 2001.
- 6 levels, low to high: **Remember - Understand - Apply - Analyze - Evaluate - Create**.
- 2001 turned the nouns into verbs; "Synthesis" became "Create" and moved to the top.
- The higher the level, the more learners must *do* with knowledge.

**Introduce**: To write measurable objectives you need a ruler, something that tells you how far "learned" goes. The ruler most educators use is Bloom's taxonomy, the 2001 version.

**Present**: I won't start with a definition. I'll start with a classroom, yours. [Show slide 6: 6 empty boxes] Picture it. You've just taught a lesson called "Asking questions in class". Six learners, six different things they can now do:

1. Lena lists the 5 types of questions I just named.
2. Marcus explains why yes/no questions don't make people think much.
3. Hana writes 3 open questions for her Year 8 maths lesson tomorrow.
4. Tomas watches a video of a lesson and points out where the questions are too easy and where they're piled on top of each other.
5. Sofia scores two question sets written by two colleagues and says which is better, and why.
6. Noah designs a set of 10 guiding questions for a lesson that's completely new to him, easy to hard.

> **Ask (pause the video)**: Who learned the "deepest"? Who the most "shallow"? Put the 6 in order. *Answer*: Lena < Marcus < Hana < Tomas < Sofia < Noah. *If your order differs*: fine, the next slide shows the logic.

[Show slide 7: staircase of 6 levels, one learner's name on each step] Those are Bloom's 6 levels:

| Level | What the learner does with knowledge | Learner |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Remember | Retrieves exactly what was given | Lena |
| 2 Understand | Restates in their own words, explains, gives examples | Marcus |
| 3 Apply | Uses it in a familiar situation | Hana |
| 4 Analyze | Breaks it apart, finds relationships, finds errors | Tomas |
| 5 Evaluate | Judges against criteria, chooses, defends the choice | Sofia |
| 6 Create | Builds something new from the parts | Noah |

The 1956 version named the levels with nouns (Knowledge, Comprehension...) and put "Synthesis" below "Evaluation". In 2001 Anderson and Krathwohl switched to verbs, because an objective describes *what the learner does*, and they moved "Create" to the top, since making something new takes the ability to judge what already exists. You only need the 2001 version. It's the one Udemy-style course design and most curriculum standards use now.

One thing matters a lot for online teachers: **not every objective should sit high**. Early lessons usually live at levels 1-2, middle ones at 3-4, and the last one, where learners build a product, at 5-6. A course made only of level 6 is one nobody can finish. A course made only of level 1? Nobody pays for it.

**Apply**: [Show slide 8: 6 new situations about "making slides"] Pause the video and sort these 6 situations into the 6 levels. One minute. [pause] Answers on slide 9.
- (a) Lists 4 types of text on a slide.
- (b) Explains why each slide should carry one idea.
- (c) Turns a 12-line slide into a 6-line slide for their own lesson.
- (d) Finds the faults in a sample slide with 3 loud colors and tiny text.
- (e) Picks the better of two slide sets for learners on phones, and gives 3 reasons.
- (f) Designs a new slide template for their own course.

Answers: (a) Remember, (b) Understand, (c) Apply, (d) Analyze, (e) Evaluate, (f) Create. Wrong on 4-5 or 5-6? Welcome to the club, those are the hardest pairs. Quick tells: Analyze is *take apart and find the fault*. Evaluate is *choose and defend with criteria*. Create is *make what didn't exist*.

**Check**: > **Ask (pause the video)**: An objective that starts "Compare two ways of opening a lesson against 3 criteria and choose the one that suits your class" is at which level? *Answer*: level 5 Evaluate (there are criteria, and a choice). Plain "compare" is level 4; adding "choose against criteria" lifts it to 5.

**Lock-in**: Three keywords go on the right strip: *6 levels · 2001 · what you do with knowledge*. Bridge: you know the levels now, so how does the sentence itself *say* the right level? With the verb. Video 3.

### 2.2 Video 3 - Action verbs for each level (10 minutes)

**Key teaching points** (slides 10-12):
- Each level has a family of verbs; the verb is **how you will check**, compressed into one word.
- Bloom 2001 verb table (M02-L1-1).
- Banned verbs: understand, know, grasp, master, be aware of, become familiar with, appreciate, realize.

**Introduce**: An objective starts with a verb, and that verb has to be something you can *see the learner do*.

**Present**: Quick test, with me. I say "learners understand Bloom's taxonomy". You're the instructor and now you have to check whether they do. What do you do? [pause] You'll ask them to *name* the 6 levels, or *explain* how two of them differ, or *sort* examples into levels. See what just happened? The moment you check, "understand" disappears and "name", "explain", "sort" take its place. **The action verb is the check, written into the objective in advance.** That's the whole reason "understand, know, grasp" are banned. Not because they're wrong. Because they don't tell anyone how you'll check. (Note that "Understand" as the name of level 2 is fine. It's a label for a level, not a verb you promise to measure.)

[Show slide 11: verb table] This table is in your downloadable sheet. For each level I picked the verbs teachers and trainers use most, up to 7 per level:

| Level | Verbs |
|---|---|
| Remember | name, list, state, identify, define, label, recall |
| Understand | describe, explain, distinguish, compare, summarize, give an example, paraphrase |
| Apply | apply, use, carry out, draft from a template, calculate, plan, present to the class |
| Analyze | analyze, determine, classify, spot errors, compare against, break down |
| Evaluate | evaluate, critique against criteria, appraise, select, conclude, defend |
| Create | design, write, build, propose, organize, develop, produce |

Two traps. Trap one: the same verb can sit at two levels, depending on what follows it. "Draft an objective from a given template" is Apply. "Draft the objectives for a new course" is Create. Trap two: in vocational and technical training documents, "describe" at the start of a knowledge objective is the standard wording, and it counts as Understand. Use it freely.

**Apply**: Pause the video and open sheet M02-L1-1. Highlight 5 verbs you've actually written in your own lesson plans. If "understand, know, grasp" show up, cross them out. Two minutes. [pause] I'll confess mine: 4 of my 5 were "know" or "understand". Yours might be worse. Fine.

**Check**: > **Ask (pause the video)**: "Learners are aware of the importance of asking questions": what's wrong, and how do you fix it? *Answer*: "be aware of" can't be measured. Fix: "Give 3 reasons why questions increase learner participation" (Understand), or "Use at least 5 open questions in a practice lesson" (Apply, observable).

**Lock-in**: *Verb = check · 6 verb families · ban understand/know/grasp*. Bridge: the verb is only one quarter of the sentence. The other three parts come in Video 4.

### 2.3 Video 4 - The 4-component formula of an objective (12 minutes)

**Key teaching points** (slides 13-17):
- **Objective = (1) Verb + (2) Object + (3) Condition, tools or context + (4) Standard.**
- (1) and (2) are mandatory; (3) and (4) are mandatory at lesson level and should be present at course level.
- Lead-in sentence: "By the end of this [course/lesson], learners will be able to:".
- 6 types of standard: accuracy; time; product quality; regulation or template; quantity or rate; correct sequence.

**Introduce**: This is the only formula you must memorize in this lesson. It comes from teacher-training course slides on program development, and it's also, roughly, how a buyer reads the "What you'll learn" box.

**Present**: [Show slide 13] Take the good objective from Video 1 and pull it apart:

| Component | In the sentence |
|---|---|
| (1) Verb | Write |
| (2) Object | a 5-minute script for a parent meeting about a child's progress |
| (3) Condition | (implied: on the script template provided; for next week's meeting) |
| (4) Standard | has opening, facts, proposal and close; ready to use |

Four parts answer four questions: *do what, with what, in what situation, to what level*. Without (3), learners don't know what they're allowed to use. Without (4), nobody knows when they're done.

[Show slide 14: 6 types of standard] The standard is the part teachers skip most, because they're afraid it sounds "rigid". There are 6 types. One or two per objective is plenty:

| Type | Example from teaching |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | sort 8/10 examples into the right Bloom level |
| Time | deliver the opening in 3-5 minutes |
| Product quality | slides with no more than 6 lines, one idea per slide |
| Regulation or template | on the center's lesson plan template; with all 4 components |
| Quantity or rate | at least 3 course objectives; 100% of lessons have an objective |
| Correct sequence | all 5 steps, no skipping the self-check |

A standard has to be something you can *see, count or compare*. "Good", "effective", "engaging", "suitable" are not standards. They're feelings. I get a little irritated every time I see "engaging" in an objective, because nobody has ever checked it.

[Show slide 15: wrong and right side by side]

| Written wrong | Fault | Rewritten |
|---|---|---|
| Understand the role of slides in a lesson | banned verb; no condition; no standard | Explain 3 roles of slides in a lesson, with one example from your own lesson for each |
| Design good slides | "good" can't be measured; no specific object | Design a set of 10 slides for a 15-minute lesson, on the Slide Master you built, one idea per slide, no more than 6 lines, readable from the back row |
| The instructor introduces principles of video design | written from the instructor's side, not the learner's | Describe 5 principles of lecture video design and identify the principle broken in each of 3 given clips, 3/3 correct |

**Apply**: [Show slide 16: 3 new faulty objectives] Pause the video and fix these three on sheet M02-L1-2, section "Practice". Three minutes. [pause] Suggested answers on slide 17. Yours don't have to match mine word for word. They need all 4 components and no banned verbs.

**Check**: > **Ask (pause the video)**: In "Write 3 objectives for your own course on the table provided, each with all 4 components", which parts are (3) and (4)? *Answer*: (3) "on the table provided"; (4) "3 objectives" and "all 4 components".

**Lock-in**: *4 components · 6 types of standard · measurable by eye*. Bridge: you have the ruler, the verbs and the formula. Now take **Quiz 1** to firm up the basics, then come to Video 5 and watch me write a whole table.

## 3. Problem solving: the process for writing an objective table, and drills

### 3.1 Video 5 (part a) - The 5-step process for a course objective table (5 minutes)

**Key teaching points** (slides 18-19): 5 steps; checkpoint after step 3; key points.

| Step | What to do | Media | Checkpoint / key point | Pass criterion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Open the course structure map (M01); for **each lesson** write one plain sentence: "after this lesson, the learner can *do what they couldn't yesterday*" | Structure map, M02-L1-2 column A | none | Every lesson has a sentence, none blank |
| 2 | Assign a Bloom level (1-6) to each sentence; check the path: low at the start of the course, high at the end | Slide 7 | **Key point**: if every lesson sits at the same level, the course is flat; push 1-2 final lessons up to levels 5-6 | At least 3 different levels in the course |
| 3 | Rewrite each sentence with the 4-component formula, picking a verb of the right level from M02-L1-1 | M02-L1-1, M02-L1-2 column B | **Checkpoint**: stop and test every sentence with 4 questions: do what - with what - in what situation - to what level; do not go to step 4 until all pass | 100% of sentences have all 4 components |
| 4 | Collect 3-5 **course-level** objectives from the lesson objectives: pick the biggest products learners have after the course | M02-L1-2 upper section | **Key point**: write *how you will check* (the product they submit) first, then step back and write the objective; an objective with no product to check gets dropped | 3-5 rows, each tied to a real product |
| 5 | Score with the 6-criterion checklist (M02-L1-3), rewrite, then submit | M02-L1-3 | none | At least 80% of objectives pass all 6 criteria, 0 banned verbs |

**Script**: [Show slide 18] Five steps, and I want you to watch two spots. The checkpoint at step 3: don't write on while any sentence is missing a component, because the mistake multiplies down the table. And the key point at step 4, which took me a few courses to figure out: **write how you will check first, the objective second**. Ask yourself "what will learners hand me?", and only then write "by the end, they can...". An objective born from a product is never vague.

### 3.2 Video 5 (part b) - Demonstration on the sample course "Asking questions in class" (7 minutes)

**Demonstration 1 (fast, 1 minute)**: [Show slide 20: structure map of the 5-lesson sample course] I run all 5 steps fast so you see the whole picture: 5 lessons → 5 plain sentences → levels 2-2-3-4-6 → rewrite with 4 components → collect 3 course objectives → score.

**Demonstration 2 (slow, explaining what - how - why)**: [Show slides 21-23]

- *Step 1, lesson 3 of the sample course "Open and closed questions"*: the plain sentence: "after this lesson the teacher can turn closed questions into open ones". Why plain words first? Because if I start with the formula, I get stuck picking a verb and forget the *real job*.
- *Step 2*: this is "do it in a familiar situation", so level 3 Apply. Why not level 2? Because she has to *change* questions, not just *explain* the difference.
- *Step 3*: "Convert 10 given closed questions into open questions (1)(2) / using the question-word list who-what-why-how (3) / all 10 can no longer be answered with yes or no (4)". I read it back with the 4 questions: do what, convert; with what, 10 closed questions; in what situation, with a question-word list; to what level, 10/10. All there. **Checkpoint passed.**
- *Step 4*: what's the biggest product after the sample course? A set of guiding questions for a real lesson. So course objective 1: "Write a set of 10 guiding questions for one of your own lessons, ordered easy to hard, with at least 6 open questions, none piled on top of another." Level 6. The other two course objectives sit at levels 2 and 4, slide 23.
- *Step 5*: I score the line I just wrote with the 6 criteria on slide 24: 6/6.

> **Ask (pause the video)**: Try **step 3** yourself for lesson 5 of the sample course ("Design a question set for a lesson"). The plain sentence is: "the teacher writes a question set for their own lesson". Turn it into a 4-component sentence. *Model answer*: "Design a set of 8-10 questions for a lesson of your choice, based on the verb table and the 3 question levels covered, with all 3 levels of recall, processing and application, ordered easy to hard." *If you could not write it*: go back to slide 13, pull apart the 4 components of the model sentence and swap in your content.

**Common mistakes**:

| Mistake | Cause | Prevention or fix |
|---|---|---|
| The objective is the name of a topic ("Bloom's taxonomy", "Types of questions") | Mixing up an objective and a table of contents | It must start with a verb; try reading "learners will... [verb]" |
| Every objective says "describe" | Playing safe with the verb | Step 2 requires at least 3 levels; the last lesson must have a product |
| The standard is "good, effective, suitable" | Fear of sounding rigid | Replace with a number, a template, a time or a sequence (slide 14) |
| Too many objectives for one lesson (6-8 lines) | Content not yet cut | Max 3 objectives per 45 minutes; move the rest to another lesson or drop it |
| Written from the instructor's side ("introduce, provide, help...") | Habit from old lesson plans | The implied subject is always the learner; delete "help learners" |

### 3.3 Task 1 - Write the objective table for your course (40 minutes, individual work)

**Task sheet** (M02-L1-2): *Who*: each learner. *What*: carry out steps 1-4 of the process for your own course (or the sample course if you have no structure map). *Where*: in the downloaded objective-table file. *How long*: 40 minutes, can be split in two sittings. *With what*: M01 structure map, M02-L1-1, slides 13-14. *Product*: a table with 3-5 course objectives and 1-2 objectives per lesson, with a Bloom level column. *Assessment*: self-score in Task 2 with the checklist M02-L1-3; the instructor responds within 7 days on the assignment.

### 3.4 Video 6 - Self-scoring with the 6-criterion checklist (9 minutes) and Task 2 (20 minutes)

**Key teaching points** (slides 24-26): the 6-criterion checklist; how to rewrite for each criterion.

| No. | Criterion | Pass indicator |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Starts with an action verb | The verb is in M02-L1-1 or equivalent; no understand, know, grasp, be aware of, become familiar with |
| 2 | Has a specific object | States what is done *to what* (product name, quantity) |
| 3 | Has a condition, tool or context | At least one: with which template, in which class, with which tool, for whom |
| 4 | Has a measurable standard | One of 6 types: number, time, observable quality, template or regulation, rate, sequence |
| 5 | Matches the planned Bloom level | Verb and object fit the level written in the "Level" column |
| 6 | Written from the learner's side, one objective one task | No "help, introduce, provide"; no "and" joining two tasks that can be checked separately |

OK, now my lock-in, following the three objectives I promised. Bloom 2001: 6 levels, and a level means what the learner does with knowledge, not how much they know. A complete objective carries a measurable verb, an object, a condition and a standard, and the verb is the check written in advance. ### 7.2 Information sheet: Bloom 2001 verb table (handout M02-L1-1)

**Purpose of the sheet**: look up verbs of the right level when writing objectives; highlight the verbs you often use.

| Level | What the learner does | Verbs (for teachers and trainers) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Remember | retrieves what was given | name, list, state, identify, define, label, recall, select the right one |
| 2 Understand | restates in their own words | describe, explain, distinguish, compare, summarize, give an example, paraphrase, illustrate |
| 3 Apply | uses it in a familiar situation | apply, use, carry out, draft from a template, calculate, plan, present to the class, convert |
| 4 Analyze | breaks apart, finds relationships, finds errors | analyze, determine, classify, spot errors, compare against, break down, map |
| 5 Evaluate | judges against criteria, chooses | evaluate, critique against criteria, appraise, select, conclude, defend, rank |
| 6 Create | makes something new | design, write, build, propose, organize, develop, produce, assemble |

**Verbs not to use**: understand, know, grasp, master, be aware of, become familiar with, appreciate, realize, have knowledge of. When you meet one, ask "what does the learner do to prove it?" and replace it with the verb of that action.
