# Teaching Methods: Choosing Them and Writing Them into the Plan

Sources: *Teaching Methods* (Nguyễn Văn Tuấn, HCMC University of Technology and Education, 2017) and the 2007 *Teaching Methodology* textbook of the same university; Arnold, Lipsmeier and Ott (1998) for the 4-step and 6-step practical models. Condensed here into rules to use while planning a lesson.

## 1. Five foundation rules

1. **No method does everything.** Each unit of content has one lead method and other methods in support; when the content changes, the method changes. A lesson that is only lecture is a failed lesson.
2. **Choose in this chain: objective -> content -> pedagogical purpose of the segment (motivate / deliver something new / consolidate / apply / check) -> learners' level -> time -> available media -> the instructor's strengths.**
3. **Alternate the class formats** whole class - group (3-7 people) - individual (10-15 minutes) within one lesson, to add up the strengths and cancel the weaknesses.
4. **Change the activity every 15 minutes.** Attention curve: the first 3-5 minutes are unfocused; minutes 5-15 are the peak; it falls gradually to minute 30; the last 15 minutes are hard to hold. About every 15 minutes there must be a "wake-up": a question, a switch to doing, watching or discussing, a change of medium.
5. **Questions are the tool that runs through every method.** See the questioning section in `engaging-delivery.md`.

## 2. Quick-choice table

| Purpose of the segment | Lead method | Suggested time | Learner activity level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Motivate, open the lesson | Problem-posing presentation; leading questions; a real object or short clip + a question | 3-7 minutes | medium |
| Teach a new concept, a hard spot | Explanation (5-10 minutes) with visuals + interleaved questions | 5-10 minutes per unit | low, so add a summary + questions |
| Tell a process, context, history, a real case | Narration (intro - body - conclusion, learners draw the conclusion) | 5-10 minutes | low to medium |
| Discover new knowledge from what is known | Guided questioning (a chain of questions) or problem-based teaching | 10-20 minutes | high |
| Learners have experience and need several viewpoints | Guided group discussion (3-7 people, ideally 3) | 15-25 minutes including reports | very high |
| Teach an operation or skill for the first time | Demonstration + 4-step practical | depends on the skill | medium to high |
| A simple skill or one with a prior base | 3-step practical | short | medium |
| Build planning and cooperation abilities | 6-step practical (with a worksheet) | long | very high |
| Build job-related decision-making | Case method (6 steps) | 30-60 minutes | very high |
| A whole problem, a finished product | Project-based teaching | several sessions | very high |
| Consolidate, check | Recall questioning; 1-minute talk; quiz; learners redo the task | 5-10 minutes | medium |

## 3. Lecturing: right place, right way

**Lecture only the core and the parts learners find hard.** Hand the rest to guided self-study (worksheets, reading) and state this in the plan.

Three forms: *explanation* (clarify a concept, 5-10 minutes, used when learners do not yet understand or have made an error); *narration* (tell, relate, describe - it needs figures, images or evidence, and learners draw their own conclusion); *extended lecture* (a complete, abstract, long topic - suits only university level, 40-60% of the time at most). More active forms: *problem-posing presentation* (arrange the material into a contradiction, then show the way to resolve it, so learners "feel as if they are taking part") and *learner presentations* (10-20 minutes + Q&A).

Every lecture segment, even 5 minutes long, must have this structure:

- **Open**: link to experience already held; state what is about to be solved.
- **Body**: split into knowledge units; plan a support technique for each (question, picture, model, example); **summarize after each unit**.
- **Close**: affirm, sharpen, point to how to apply it, pose the next question.

Numbers for a self-check: speaking at 100-200 words per minute means 12,000 words an hour, while short-term memory holds only 800-1,000 words. So: go fast where the material is easy or already known, slow where it is new or hard; **pause a few seconds after an important sentence**; short sentences, spoken style; vary volume and pace; move around the room and look at each person; do not read the plan aloud.

When writing the lecture script inside the content file: write exactly as it will be spoken, short sentences, with markers such as *[pause]*, *[ask the class]*, *[show slide n]*, *[write on board]*.

## 4. Demonstration (teaching an operation)

Preparation: seat learners in a U or V shape so **everyone can see**; put them in a warm-up situation; state the purpose and the key points to watch; have leading questions ready; choose a typical sample; rehearse until fluent; have enough equipment including backups; safety.

Run it as **3 demonstrations**:

1. Do it at **normal speed** so learners see the whole picture.
2. Do it **slowly, step by step, explaining what - how - why**; repeat the hard steps; ask as you go ("Why am I doing it this way? What will I do next?"); you may make a deliberate mistake at one step to test them; you may let a learner "direct" you.
3. Do it again at **normal speed** with a summary.

After the demonstration, at least one learner must redo one stage before the class moves to practice. Learners watch what the teacher does more than what the teacher says, so even small habits (arranging tools, switching the machine off afterwards) are part of what is taught.

## 5. Guided questioning (ask - answer)

Two kinds: *recall* (review, consolidation, checking) and *leading* (a chain of questions toward new knowledge; a problem-posing variant starts from a problem situation). Overuse of recall does not develop thinking; too many questions break the plan.

Required technique: **ask the whole class -> wait (3-5 seconds of silence) -> nominate -> invite another learner to comment or add -> the instructor locks in the answer**. Do not mechanically repeat a learner's answer. Spread answerers evenly (name cards). Give the hard questions to strong learners but lead step by step so weaker ones can follow. Every question in the plan must **carry its expected answer**.

Handling the 4 cases: correct -> praise, acknowledge; partly correct -> affirm the correct part, invite additions; wrong -> acknowledge the effort, **correct the answer, not the person**, invite someone else; no answer -> make no fuss, ask another person, rephrase the question, use an image then ask again.

Eight probing moves to dig into thinking: silence; encouragement ("keep going"); detail ("say more"); clarification ("do you mean..."); challenge ("if that is true, what happens when..."); evidence ("what is that based on..."); relevance ("how does that apply here..."); example ("give one concrete example").

## 6. Group discussion

Use only when learners **already have the knowledge, experience and materials**; the issue must be an **open one** (several directions possible). Four types: guided (the most common), seminar, round table (calm, no criticism), debate (with rebuttal, tense).

Preparation: a clear objective (what learners will be able to do with the ideas gathered); the task written on the board or sheet; an introduction + a challenging question (answerable only in part); the number of groups (more groups cost more reporting time); the reporting format; groups of 3-7, ideally 3; groups seated so they can see each other's faces.

Running it: do not answer for the class ("Before I give my view, I want to hear yours"); write the key points on the board; draw in the shy ("Do you want to add anything to that?") and hold back the over-eager; do not argue with a wrong idea right away, leave it for the end of the session; keep conclusions anonymous; **do not let learners take notes** during the discussion; sum up each part, then the whole at the end.

Group lesson process in 7 steps: raise awareness -> analyze the problem -> assign the task -> plan -> group work -> agree the result (whole class) -> consolidate. The task assignment must cover who - does what - where - how long - with what - what product - how it will be presented or assessed.

## 7. Teaching practical skills

A skill forms in 3 stages: **grasp the knowledge -> imitate (build the movement pattern) -> practice (the skill)**; the instructor correspondingly **orients -> demonstrates -> coaches**. A practical lesson always has 3 phases of guidance: **opening - ongoing - closing** (finish the practice *before* the end of the session so there is time for comments).

Three models:

- **4 steps** (first-time skill teaching): Information (get attention, task, quality standard, check preparation) -> Demonstration + explanation of what / how / why -> Learner redoes it and self-explains, the instructor asks check questions and corrects -> Self-practice, the instructor steps in when needed, checks against the standard.
- **3 steps** (a simple skill or one with a base): Information -> Theory + procedure + grouping + safety -> Practice.
- **6 steps** (to develop independent planning and cooperation; needs a worksheet): Information -> Plan (the group makes it) -> Decision (talk it over with the instructor) -> Execution -> Check (with the sheet) -> Evaluation (what to do better next time).

The opening guidance must **check the related theory** before learners start; stress checkpoints, key points, the usual types of fault and how to fix them; assign positions and time allowances; safety.

## 8. Problem-based teaching

The core is the **problem situation**, not the question. A good situation has: need or interest; both the known and the unknown; solvable with the learners' own strength. Six ways to create one: **a sudden change, a surprise, a mismatch, a conflict, a refutation, a choice**.

The 4-step process to write into the plan: Pose the problem (task, situation, purpose) -> Study the problem (collect what learners know, read materials) -> Solve it (propose solutions, pick the best option) -> Apply to a similar situation. Three levels of learner independence: independent research; partial exploration (through questioning); presented problem solving (the instructor solves it aloud, with fumbling, failures and adjustments, which still grips learners more than a ready-made result).

**Case method**: the case comes from real practice in the trade, is complex, contains conflict, has several solutions and suits the learners' level. Six steps: recognize the situation -> gather information -> look for options -> decide within the group -> defend -> compare with the real decision. Four types: find the hidden problem (with noise), solve a stated problem, find the missing information yourself, critique the options given.

**Project**: a real product with real use value; learners choose the topic within the direction the instructor suggests; SMART objectives; a task breakdown; a Gantt chart; assess both the process and the product. Use it only when the program is flexible enough and has many sessions.

## 9. How to write methods into the lesson plan

In the sequence table, the "Instructor activity" column holds an **action verb + method/technique + medium**, never the bare name of a method. Wrong: "Lecture". Right: "Explain concept X with the 3-block diagram on slide 4; ask: '...?' (answer: ...); sum up in one sentence on the right side of the board."

The "Learner activity" column holds **observable actions**: listen and note 3 keywords; answer; discuss in pairs for 2 minutes and write it down; redo steps 2-3; cross-mark using the sheet. Do not write "listen and absorb" on every line.
