# Making a Lesson Engaging

Every technique here is grounded in teaching-methodology sources: the attention curve, motivation, problem situations, presentation technique and feedback. In vocational and professional training, "engaging" does not mean entertaining. It means **holding attention and pulling learners into action** for the whole lesson. Use this file when you write the lesson content file (the spoken script, the examples, the questions).

## 1. Pacing the lesson along the attention curve

- Minutes 0-5: learners have not settled yet. Open with an **action or a question**, never with "Today we are going to learn about...".
- Minutes 5-15: attention peaks. Put the **hardest material and the core concept** here.
- Minutes 15-30: attention drops. Move to **doing**: examples, a short exercise, pair discussion, a demonstration.
- Last 15 minutes: hardest to hold. **Change the format completely**: a game, a role play, a one-minute presentation, a quiz, a small product.
- The 15-minute rule: no stretch where learners only listen for more than 15 minutes. In a 45-minute lesson, change the method at least 3 times. For self-paced video, one video covers one unit and runs 10-12 minutes at most.

## 2. Motivating lead-ins (4 source methods plus variants)

The sources give four ways to motivate: **activate prior experience; create a problem situation; stir emotion and show why the topic matters for the job; create a conflict with what learners already believe.** Write a lead-in of 3-5 minutes, pick one method, and always finish by **stating the objective as a concrete promise** ("By the end of these 45 minutes you will have in your hands...").

Variants (the examples below are illustrative. Replace the setting with the job world of the course's target learners):
1. **A real case or classroom story**: "Last Thursday Maria, who teaches Year 9 science in Leeds, sent me a 12-page lesson plan and asked why her students were still falling asleep..." Give it a person, a time, a detail, and leave it unresolved.
2. **A striking number with a source**: "An hour of lecture runs to roughly 9,000 words. Short-term memory keeps a fraction of that. So where do the rest go?" Use only figures you can source.
3. **The 6 kinds of problem situation**: *sudden turn* (the result is the opposite of what was expected), *surprise*, *mismatch* (theory does not match practice), *conflict* (two ways of working both make sense), *refutation* (state a popular belief, then take it apart), *choice* (the learner must pick one of two or three options).
4. **Try before you learn**: let learners do a quick version of the task you are about to teach (a nurse educator in Manila asks each person to write one learning objective in 2 minutes), then inspect the errors. Their own mistakes are the motivation.
5. **A real object or a 1-2 minute clip plus a question** posed *before* viewing. A sales trainer in Austin plays a 90-second recorded cold call and asks: "Where did the prospect decide to hang up?"
6. **Lightning survey**: each person gives one sentence about related experience.

Avoid: opening with a definition, reading out the table of contents, "as you all know", and any lead-in longer than 5 minutes.

## 3. Explaining so it sticks

- **Example first, concept second** (inductive) for new content. Concept first, example second (deductive) for review or extension. Give every concept at least one correct example and one wrong or "almost right" example for contrast.
- **Examples must come from the learners' own work world**, as fixed in the course's market research or target learner description. For teachers: classes, students, parents, lesson plans, slides. For any other trade: that trade's tasks, clients and tools. Do not borrow an example from another profession when one from theirs exists.
- **Structured narration**: introduction, body, conclusion; with figures, images and evidence; end with a question so learners draw the conclusion themselves instead of hearing it handed over.
- **Problem-posing presentation**: instead of announcing the result, retell the road to it, including the groping around and the failures.
- **One sentence, one idea.** Short, spoken sentences. Slow down on what is new, speed up on what is familiar. After an important sentence: *[pause 3 seconds]*.
- **Summarize after each knowledge unit** in 1-2 sentences or 3 keywords on the board; summarize the whole lesson at the end.
- Vary voice, intensity and tempo; move around the room; keep eye contact; use humor in moderation; never read the plan aloud.
- State the "why" before the "how" in every procedure. During a demonstration ask: "Why am I doing it this way? What will I do next?"
- Lecture only the core. What learners can read themselves becomes an active-reading task with guiding questions.

## 4. Questions: written in advance, with answers

Every activity in the content file needs prepared questions **with the expected answer and a fallback if nobody responds**. A 45-minute lesson should carry 8-15 prepared questions.

Requirements: they provoke thinking (avoid yes/no, and if you use one, follow with "why"); short and clear, one idea per question; well timed; pitched to the learners' level; ordered easy to hard, known to unknown, cause to effect; no chained questions.

Seven types to mix: identifying (5W1H); choosing and justifying; open-ended (list, describe, prove, compare, analyze, synthesize); evaluating (comment, give your own view); curiosity-raising ("What happens if...?"); pulling in the distracted ("Can you sum up the last point?"); leading into the new lesson (systematic, in sequence).

Technique: ask the whole group, wait 3-5 seconds, name someone, invite another person to comment, then lock in. Correct the answer, not the person; never mock; probe with silence, "say more", "for example?", "what is your evidence?".

## 5. Keeping learners doing

- Every 10-15 minutes, a small task: write 3 keywords; a 2-minute Think-Pair-Share; a show-of-hands vote; a lightning round; inspect a real sample; redo one step.
- Every medium you put in front of learners comes with a job: watch the clip and answer the 2 questions given beforehand; receive the sheet and fill it in; look at a sample lesson plan and find 2 errors.
- Give tasks with all 7 elements: who, what, where, how long, with what, the product, how it is judged.
- Use the learners' own experience as material: they bring their own lesson plans, slides and videos, and the exercises run on their documents.
- Positive feedback: specific, timely, not judging the person's worth, pointing to options. Let learners give each other feedback with techniques such as 3-by-3 and the gallery walk.

## 6. A closing that lingers

A 3-7 minute close contains: (1) learners summarize for themselves (a one-minute talk, 3 keywords, a quick mind map) before the instructor locks in; (2) the instructor sharpens the summary and corrects the errors heard along the way; (3) a bridge to the next lesson and to real work ("Tonight, apply this to tomorrow's lesson plan"); (4) an assignment with a product, a deadline and criteria; (5) one sentence that leaves a thought or an open question behind. Never close with "That's it. Any questions?".

## 7. The voice of the script

- Write the **spoken lecture exactly as it will be said**: first person, "I" and "you", short sentences, pauses, questions, and action cues in square brackets.
- Do not write in textbook voice ("Concept X is understood to be..."). Write as the person at the front of the room does ("Look at this line. What is missing?").
- Each activity runs: *purpose (one sentence), lead-in, what learners do, question plus answer, lock-in, bridge to the next activity*.
- Keep terminology consistent with the learning objectives and with the program.

## 8. Engagement self-check before handing over

For a self-paced video course, read the lines below in these equivalents: "prepared questions" means pause-the-video questions whose answers are spoken after the pause; "organizational formats" means watch, answer, do, submit, Q&A; "learners summarize first" means writing 3 keywords in the Q&A or on a worksheet before hearing the lock-in; "continuous listening stretch" means a single video with no pause point.

- [ ] The lead-in is an action, question or situation, runs 5 minutes or less, and ends with a concrete promise.
- [ ] No continuous listening stretch exceeds 15 minutes; each stretch ends with a summary.
- [ ] At least 8 prepared questions with answers; at least 1 problem situation.
- [ ] Every concept has a correct example and a wrong example, both from the target learners' job world.
- [ ] Every medium has a learner task attached.
- [ ] At least 2 organizational formats (whole group plus pair, group or individual).
- [ ] The close has learners summarizing first, and a take-home assignment with a product, a deadline and criteria.
- [ ] Every minute of content serves at least one line of the learning objectives.
