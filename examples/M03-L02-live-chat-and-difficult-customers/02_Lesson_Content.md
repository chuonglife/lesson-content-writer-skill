## 1. Lead-in (5 min)

**Purpose**: problem situation of the sudden-turn kind. Learners expect that yesterday's 5-part reply works everywhere; the transcript shows a correct, polite agent losing a customer on chat. Links to the reply structure they learned in Lesson 1 and to the Rotaplane rota feature from M02.

[Show slide 2: the transcript, timestamps visible.]

This is a chat from our own queue, names changed. Read it. Don't say anything yet.

> 14:02:10 Joanne Reyes (Pickering Vets, Team plan): Our rota's gone. Completely blank for next week. I have 11 vets and nurses asking me what they're working and surgery opens in 40 minutes.
>
> 14:04:50 Ben (agent): Hi Joanne, thank you for contacting Pellucid Support! I'm sorry to hear you're having trouble with your rota. I completely understand how frustrating this must be. Rotaplane rotas can appear blank for several reasons, including filters on the schedule view, unpublished drafts or location settings. To help me look into this, could you please confirm which location you're viewing, whether you published the rota, and whether this affects the mobile app, the web app or both? Kind regards, Ben
>
> 14:05:30 Joanne: web. both. I don't know. it WAS there yesterday
>
> 14:09:40 Ben: Thank you for confirming. Unfortunately, as I mentioned, there are several possible causes. I have checked your account and the rota is saved as a draft. Drafts are not visible to staff. To make it visible please go to Schedule, Week of 16 Nov, Publish. Is there anything else I can help you with today?
>
> 14:10:05 Joanne: So it's my fault. Brilliant. How do I cancel.

[Pause. Let them finish reading.]

Here's the annoying part. Ben was right. The rota was a draft, the fix was correct, it took one click. He used a greeting, he acknowledged, he gave steps, he had a next step and a sign-off. All 5 parts. And Joanne wants to cancel.

> **Ask**: Hands up when I point at the moment you think Joanne was lost. [Point at each timestamp in turn.] *Expected answer*: votes split, usually between 14:04:50 (the wall of text after a long wait) and 14:09:40 ("as I mentioned"). *If nobody answers*: put your own hand up at 14:04:50 and say "two minutes forty before the first word, and then a paragraph". Then ask who disagrees.

> **Ask**: Ben's fix was right. So what was wrong? *Expected answer*: the time and the way it was said; too slow, too long, three questions at once, it sounded like blame. *If nobody answers*: "Read his last message as if you were Joanne, with 11 people staring at you. What do you hear?" Someone will say "it's your fault".

[Show slide 3: the same chat with the gaps highlighted in red.]

Good. That's the lesson. Email forgives you. Chat doesn't.

## 3. Problem solving

### 3.1 Live chat pacing and multitasking (14 min)

**Key teaching points** (slide 5 and the left zone of the board):
- First reply within 60 seconds
- No silence over 2 minutes: send a holding message
- One idea per message, 3 lines or fewer
- 2 chats at a time at most in your first month
- 3 tabs: chat, customer account, knowledge base

**Introduce**: chat runs on the customer's clock, not ours; these 4 rules come from the Pellucid playbook and each one would have saved Ben's chat.

**Present** (7 min):

[Point at the 14:02:10 and 14:04:50 timestamps on slide 3.]

Two minutes forty. That's how long Joanne sat looking at an empty chat window. On email, two minutes forty is nothing, nobody even knows. On chat the customer is watching the little dots. Every second with no dots is a second they spend building a worse story in their head.

So rule one. First reply within 60 seconds. And the first reply does not need to fix anything. It needs to show a human read the message. "Hi Joanne, a blank rota 40 minutes before opening, I'm on it now." That's it. Eleven words, sent at 14:02:40 instead of 14:04:50.

[Write on the left zone: 60 s.]

Rule two. No silence over 2 minutes. When you go off to check the account or read a knowledge base article, you send a holding message first, and it says what you are doing, not "one moment please". I can't stand "one moment please". One moment of what? A holding message sounds like this: "Checking your schedule settings now, back in under 2 minutes." Then you come back before the 2 minutes, even if it's only to say "found it, one more check".

The playbook says 2 minutes. I do it differently, I aim for 90 seconds, because the playbook number is the limit and I never want to sit on the limit. You'll be scored on 2 minutes. Aim for 90.

Rule three. One idea per message, 3 lines or fewer in the chat widget. Look at Ben's first message. Seven lines on Joanne's screen and three questions in one go. She answered "web. both. I don't know." Of course she did. She picked one and guessed the rest.

I got this wrong myself. My first month on chat, a customer called Greg who runs a garage in Swindon asked why his payroll export was empty and I sent him a 9-line answer with every possible cause. He replied with one word. "What?" Then I sent it again in 4 short messages and he fixed it in a minute. Same words. Different chunks.

Rule four. Two chats at a time, maximum, for your first month. Ticketyard will happily give you a third. Don't take it. Put yourself on "Busy" when you have two. And when both chats need you at once, tell one of them, with a time: "I'm with you in 90 seconds, Hamid." Customers forgive a stated wait. They don't forgive a silent one.

[Show slide 6: the 3-tab setup.]

Multitasking is mostly about tabs. Tab one, the chat. Tab two, the customer's account in the sandbox admin, open before you type the first reply, so you already know their plan and how many locations they have. Tab three, the knowledge base. If you're hunting for tabs while the customer waits, the 2-minute rule breaks itself. And type your internal note as you go, not at the end. At the end you've forgotten half of it.

**Apply** (5 min): Think-Pair-Share. Pairs take sheet M03-L2-2, part A: a timestamped chat log between agent Mo and Hamid Rahimi of Rahimi Bakehouse. Task: in 3 minutes, mark every place a pacing rule is broken and write the rule number next to it. Then 2 minutes: each pair swaps sheets with the pair beside them and compares. Product: a marked log. The 4 planted breaks are listed in the answer key under sheet M03-L2-2.

[Call out the time at 1 minute left.]

**Check** (2 min):

> **Ask**: Pair at the back, which break did you find last, and which rule is it? *Answer*: usually break 4, Mo accepting a third chat at 09:14:30 (rule 4); the others are the 1 minute 35 first reply (rule 1), the 3 minute 20 silence (rule 2) and the 6-line message (rule 3). *If nobody answers*: read the 09:14:30 line out loud and ask "how many chats is Mo holding now?"

> **Ask**: Write a holding message for Hamid at 09:13:05 that would have fixed the silence. *Answer*: something with what Mo is doing and a time, for example "Checking your tablet's PIN settings now, back within 2 minutes." *If nobody answers*: write the first half on the board, "Checking your...", and ask the room to finish it.

**Lock-in**: [Write on the right zone: 60 s / holding message / one idea per message.] Read them out: first reply in 60 seconds. [pause 3 seconds] A holding message that says what you are doing and when you'll be back. [pause 3 seconds] One idea per message. [pause 3 seconds]

*Bridge*: Speed keeps the customer from getting angrier. It doesn't bring them back down. For that you need the sequence.

### 3.2 De-escalation sequence (15 min)

**Key teaching points** (slides 7-8 and the left zone of the board):
- 1 Hear it: their problem, their words, their number
- 2 Own the next move: what you are doing right now
- 3 Narrow it down: one question at a time
- 4 Commit and confirm: a result or a time, then check with them
- One apology at most; one boundary line if insulted

**Introduce**: an angry customer has two problems, the broken thing and the feeling that nobody is dealing with it; the 4-step Pellucid sequence handles the second one first, in about two messages.

**Present** (8 min):

[Show slide 7. Read Joanne's first message out loud, with feeling.]

Joanne's message has three facts in it. Blank rota. 11 vets and nurses. 40 minutes to opening. Ben's reply repeated none of them. He wrote "I'm sorry to hear you're having trouble with your rota". That could go to anybody about anything.

Step one, Hear it. You say her problem back in her words, with her number. "A blank rota with 11 people waiting and 40 minutes to go, that's awful timing." Now she knows a person read it. That's the only job of step one.

[Write 1 Hear it in the left zone.]

> **Ask**: Why her number, why not just "I understand your frustration"? *Expected answer*: because the number proves you read her message; "I understand your frustration" is a macro anyone could paste. *If nobody answers*: ask "If I wrote 'I understand your frustration' to all 6 of your customers today, would any of them feel heard?"

Step two, Own the next move. Say what you're doing right now. Not what you'd like to do, not a policy, not a question. "I'm opening your schedule now." The customer stops pushing when they can see somebody else has the wheel. Steps one and two often go in the same short message, or two quick ones, inside the 60 seconds.

Step three, Narrow it down. One question at a time, and a closed one where you can. Ben asked three questions in one message. Try "Is it next week, the week of the 16th?" Yes. "Are you looking at the web app right now?" Yes. Each answer is one tap for her and one fact for you. You've got your diagnostic questions from M02 for login, billing and integrations; you just ask them one by one.

Step four, Commit and confirm. You give a result or a time, and you keep it. "Found it. It's saved as a draft, so your team can't see it yet. I'll walk you through publishing, it's one button." Then you confirm with her: "Can you refresh and tell me if you see Monday?" You don't close until she says yes. That's the M02 rule as well, verify the fix with the customer before closing.

[Write 2 Own, 3 Narrow, 4 Commit in the left zone.]

Two extras. Apologies: one per chat, max. One clean "Sorry about this" is fine. Five of them read like panic, and panic makes customers angrier. And insults. If someone calls you useless, and today someone will, you don't defend yourself and you don't apologise again. You use one boundary line from the playbook: "I want to fix this for you, and I can do it faster if we stick to the rota." Once. If the abuse carries on after that, you tell them you're bringing in your team lead and you hand over. The full handover is M04. Today: one boundary line, that's all.

[Show slide 8: Ben's chat rewritten with the 4 steps, 5 short messages, timestamps ending at 14:06:10.]

Same fix as Ben. Four minutes earlier. And nobody asked how to cancel.

**Apply** (5 min): individual, sheet M03-L2-2 part B. On screen (slide 9) and on the sheet is an opener from Simone Albrecht of Albrecht Physio, written in capitals. Task: write the first 2 messages you would send, using step 1 and step 2, each message 3 lines or fewer. 3 minutes writing, then compare with the person beside you for 2 minutes and mark which step each message is. Product: 2 messages on the sheet.

**Check** (2 min):

> **Ask**: Read me your first message. [Pick two learners.] Class, which step is it, and did it use her number? *Answer*: a good one says the problem back with her detail, for example "A cancellation text to all 38 of your staff for tomorrow, I can see why you're furious." It is step 1 and uses the 38. *If nobody answers*: read the sample from the answer key and ask the room to find the number in it.

> **Ask**: Simone writes back "AND WHO IS GOING TO PHONE 38 PEOPLE, YOU?" Which step are you on now? *Answer*: still step 2 going into 3: own the next move ("I'm checking right now whether the text was sent or only queued"), then narrow with one question. Not another apology. *If nobody answers*: ask "has anything changed for her since your first message?" No. So she still needs to see somebody acting.

**Lock-in**: [Point at the left zone.] Read out each step and pause: Hear it, their problem, their words, their number. [pause 3 seconds] Own the next move, say what you're doing now. [pause 3 seconds] Narrow it down, one question at a time. [pause 3 seconds] Commit and confirm, a result or a time, then check with them. [pause 3 seconds]

*Bridge*: Knowing the steps is half of it. The other half is not saying the five or six lines that undo them in one go.

### 3.3 Fuel lines (10 min)

**Key teaching points** (slide 10, right zone of the board):
- Fuel line: a true sentence that makes the customer angrier
- Test 1: no blame word (you, should, already, as I said)
- Test 2: no policy before help
- Test 3: 3 lines or fewer

**Introduce**: some lines are polite, true and still pour fuel on the fire; you will meet all 6 on this sheet in your first week on the queue.

**Present** (2 min):

I'll be blunt. There is one line I never want to see in a Pellucid transcript, and it's "Calm down". Nobody in the history of chat has calmed down because they were told to. I've seen it in our own queue 3 times this year, and all 3 ended in a complaint to the team lead.

A fuel line is a sentence that's true but makes things worse. "As I already said" is true. It also says "you weren't listening". "Unfortunately that's our policy" is true. It also says "I'm not going to try". Lauren on the billing queue keeps a list of them taped to her monitor, and the 6 on your sheet come from her list.

**Apply** (6 min): Say it another way, in trios. Count off 1 to 4 round the room; same numbers sit together, 4 trios. Each trio gets sheet M03-L2-3: 6 fuel lines. Task: rewrite each line so it passes the 3 tests on the sheet; one person writes, one checks against the tests, the third reads each rewrite aloud as the customer would hear it. 5 minutes. Then each trio reads out its best rewrite, 15 seconds each, 1 minute total. Product: 6 rewrites, each with a tick or cross per test. Criterion: at least 5 of 6 pass all 3 tests (objective S1).

**Check** (2 min):

> **Ask**: Line 4 is "You should have checked your plan before buying." Trio 2, read your rewrite. Class, which test does the original fail? *Answer*: test 1, blame words "you should have"; a rewrite that passes is "Swap approvals are on the Team plan. On Starter you can still swap shifts by hand, I'll show you." *If nobody answers*: underline "you should have" on the board and ask "who is that sentence about, the problem or the customer?"

> **Ask**: Line 5 is three apologies in a row. Why is it a fuel line when it's so polite? *Answer*: because it shows panic and says nothing about the fix; the customer still doesn't know anyone is acting. One apology plus step 2. *If nobody answers*: read it aloud in a shaky voice; people laugh, then someone says "it sounds scared".

**Lock-in**: [Right zone: fuel line / 3 tests.] Read out: a fuel line is true and still makes it worse. [pause 3 seconds] No blame word, no policy before help, 3 lines or fewer. [pause 3 seconds]

*Bridge*: Ten-minute break. When you come back, Ruth and I are going to do this for real, on the projector, and you're going to watch me break the job sheet's checkpoints if I get careless.

[Break, 10 minutes, not counted.]

### 3.4 Procedure and demonstration: handle a hot chat (15 min)

#### Procedure (job sheet M03-L2-4)

| Step | What to do | Tools and media | Checkpoint / key point | Pass criterion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Accept the chat; read the whole first message and the account sidebar (plan, locations, recent changes) | Ticketyard agent console; tab 2 sandbox admin | Checkpoint: you know the business name and plan before you type | Reading done within 30 seconds |
| 2 | Send the first reply: Hear it plus Own the next move, in 1 or 2 short messages | Chat | Key point: say their problem back with their own number | First reply sent within 60 seconds of the chat landing |
| 3 | If you need to see or change account detail, start the identity check | Ticketyard Verify button (sends a 6-digit code to the account owner's email) | Checkpoint: no account detail shared or changed until the code matches | Code confirmed before any account detail |
| 4 | Narrow it down: ask one question at a time, using the M02 diagnostic questions; send a holding message before every lookup | Chat; tab 3 knowledge base | Key point: closed questions where you can; aim for 90 seconds between messages | No gap over 2 minutes |
| 5 | Fix it or walk the customer through the fix, one step per message | Sandbox admin; knowledge base article | Checkpoint: the fix matches the knowledge base article for this issue | Issue resolved within 10 minutes of the chat landing |
| 6 | Commit and confirm: say what is done, ask the customer to check it on their side | Chat | Key point: do not close until the customer confirms | Customer confirms the fix in the chat |
| 7 | Close and note: sign off; write the internal note (issue, fix, heat at start and end) and tag per the M01 rules | Internal note; tags | Checkpoint: note complete before accepting the next chat | No field blank; tags match the M01 tag list |

#### Demonstration

[Ruth sits at the back with the customer widget, demo card D0 in hand. My agent console is on the projector. Timer on screen.]

Before we start, two things to watch for. [Write in the middle zone.] One: how long before my first message lands? Two: count my questions, how many per message? Keep your own tally.

Run 1, normal speed (5 min). Ruth plays Nadia Haddad of Haddad Pharmacy, heat 4: a new pharmacist can't see any shifts on her phone. I run the chat start to finish in real time, without commentary, steps 1 to 7.

> **Ask** (after run 1): How long was my first reply, and how many questions did I ever put in one message? *Expected answer*: about 40 seconds; one question per message, every time. *If nobody answers*: scroll up to the timestamps on the projector and let them read it off the screen.

Run 2, slow, with what, how and why (5 min). I scroll back through the transcript message by message.

Look at my first message. "Hi Nadia, your new pharmacist can't see a single shift and she starts Thursday, I'm looking at your locations now." What: steps 1 and 2 in one message. How: I took "new pharmacist" and "Thursday" from her message. Why: so she knows I read it, before I ask her anything.

Next. I needed to look at the staff member's profile, which is account detail. So before anything else I pressed Verify and said "I've sent a 6-digit code to the account owner's email, can you read it to me?" Why there? Because the very next thing I'm going to do is open a person's record. Step 3 goes where the detail starts, not at the end.

> **Ask**: What will I do next, after she reads the code back? *Expected answer*: open the staff profile and ask one narrowing question, or send a holding message before the lookup. *If nobody answers*: point at step 4 on the job sheet.

Here, at 10:21:40. "Checking which location she's assigned to, back within 2 minutes." That's my holding message. I came back at 10:23:00. Eighty seconds.

And watch this one. At 10:24:10 I nearly closed. I'd fixed it, she'd been added to Haddad Pharmacy Old Town, I typed "Is there anything else?" Then I deleted it and wrote "Can you ask her to pull down to refresh and tell me if Thursday shows?" Why? Because a fix I've done in admin isn't a fix until the pharmacist sees her shift.

Run 3, summary (1 min). [Point at the job sheet on slide 11.] Read, reply in 60 with their number, verify before detail, one question at a time with holding messages, fix by the article, confirm on their side, note before the next chat.

**Learners try it** (3 min): three learners (25% of the class) come to the projected console. Ruth sends a fresh opener from Simone Albrecht. Learner 1 types the step 2 first reply. Learner 2 types the holding message for a lookup (step 4). Learner 3 types the commit-and-confirm line (step 6). The room checks each one against the job sheet's pass criterion. Pass level: can do it, the message meets the criterion with at most one prompt.

**Common mistakes**:

| Mistake | Cause | Prevention / fix |
|---|---|---|
| First reply after 60 seconds | Trying to diagnose before saying anything | Send steps 1 and 2 first, look things up after |
| Three questions in one message | Copying the email habit from Lesson 1 | One question, wait for the answer, next question |
| Silence while reading the article | Forgetting the holding message | Holding message first, then open tab 3 |
| Account detail shared before Verify | Customer pushes and sounds genuine | Verify every time; say "this protects your staff's data" |
| Closing before the customer checks | Fix looks done in admin | Ask them to check on their side; wait for the yes |
| A second and third apology | Panic when heat rises | One apology, then step 2 again: say what you are doing |

### 3.5 Practice: 6 role-played chats (80 min)

**Task brief** (read out and on slide 12):
- Who: pairs, A and B, the pairs from 3.1. Partners sit back to back so they can't see or hear each other's reactions.
- What: 6 rounds. In rounds 1, 3 and 5, A is the agent and B plays the customer. In rounds 2, 4 and 6 they swap. B holds cards C1, C3, C5; A holds cards C2, C4, C6.
- Where: at your own laptop; the agent works in the Ticketyard agent console, the customer types in the customer chat widget.
- How long: each round is 13 minutes: 1 minute for the customer to read the card, 10 minutes of chat with a hard stop when the timer sounds, 2 minutes to score. 6 rounds = 78 minutes, plus 2 minutes to set up = 80.
- With what: scenario cards (sheet M03-L2-6), job sheet M03-L2-4, check sheet M03-L2-5, the knowledge base.
- Product: 3 agent transcripts per learner in Ticketyard, each with a complete internal note.
- How assessed: after each round, the customer player gives the end heat (1-5) and both fill in the check sheet for that round. Facilitators score all 3 agent transcripts of every learner on the check sheet. Pass on S2, A1 and A2: all 3 transcripts meet every criterion.

[Round timetable in the middle zone of the board: R1 A agent C1 / R2 B agent C2 / R3 A agent C3 / R4 B agent C4 / R5 A agent C5 / R6 B agent C6.]

Customer players, a word for you. The card tells you who you are, what happened, your opening message, your starting heat and what moves your heat up or down. After the opening message, you make it up. Don't help your agent. Don't be cruel either. Play the card. If the agent does what lowers your heat, let it go down. When the timer goes, you tell your agent your end heat, out loud, and they write it in the internal note.

**How support fades**:
- Rounds 1-2: job sheet open on the desk. Ruth and I walk the room, read over shoulders and may stop a chat for 20 seconds to point at a step.
- Rounds 3-4: job sheet face down. We only answer "where is the article for this" questions.
- Rounds 5-6: no help. Ruth and I score from the supervisor view, 3 agents each per round.

**What the instructor watches for**: first reply time on the supervisor view; three-question messages; long silences while the agent reads an article; account detail before Verify (card C4 is built to test this); the agent's reply right after the insult in C4 and the capitals in C6; closing before confirmation.

**Debrief between rounds 2 and 3, and between 4 and 5** (inside the 2-minute scoring slots, whole room, 30 seconds each):

> **Ask** (after round 2): Customers, put a hand up if your heat went up at any point. What did the agent write just before? *Expected answer*: a policy line, a long message, a silence, "as I said". *If nobody answers*: name what you saw on the supervisor view without naming the agent: "I saw one first reply land at 2 minutes 10."

> **Ask** (after round 4): Agents who had card C4, what did you do when he asked you to just read out the email on the account? *Expected answer*: refused politely and used Verify. *If nobody answers*: ask "what does the job sheet say at step 3?"

### 7.2 Information sheet M03-L2-1: Live chat rules and the de-escalation sequence (M03 Lesson 2, cohort and date: ............)

**Pacing rules (Pellucid playbook)**
1. Send the first reply within 60 seconds of the chat landing. It shows the message was read and says what happens next; it does not need to fix anything.
2. Do not leave the customer more than 2 minutes without a message. Before any lookup, send a holding message stating the action and the time: "Checking your schedule settings now, back within 2 minutes."
3. Write one idea per message, 3 lines or fewer in the chat widget. Ask one question per message.
4. Hold 2 chats at most during the first month. Set status to Busy at 2. When both chats need attention, tell one customer the wait in seconds or minutes.

**Three-tab setup**: tab 1 the chat; tab 2 the customer's account in the sandbox admin, opened before the first reply; tab 3 the knowledge base. Write the internal note during the chat.

**De-escalation sequence (Pellucid playbook)**
| Step | What it does | Example line |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Hear it | Says the problem back in the customer's words, with their own detail or number | "A blank rota with 11 people waiting and 40 minutes to go." |
| 2 Own the next move | States what the agent is doing right now | "I'm opening your schedule now." |
| 3 Narrow it down | Asks one question at a time, closed where possible, from the M02 diagnostic questions | "Is it the week of the 16th?" |
| 4 Commit and confirm | Gives a result or a time, then asks the customer to check on their side | "It's published. Can you refresh and tell me if you see Monday?" |

**Apologies**: one per chat at most.
**Insults**: one boundary line: "I want to fix this for you, and I can do it faster if we stick to the [problem]." If the abuse continues, tell the customer the team lead is joining and hand over (procedure in M04).
**Identity check**: before sharing or changing any account detail, press Verify in Ticketyard and ask the customer to read back the 6-digit code sent to the account owner's email.
